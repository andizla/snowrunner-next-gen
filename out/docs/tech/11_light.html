Contact shadows

Card: Contact shadows. Fine sun shadows traced on screen where things touch or overlap: under a tyre, where a log lies on another, the creases of a cab.

Why the shadow map misses them

A shadow texel covers centimetres to decimetres of ground, and the depth bias that prevents acne pushes the comparison back further. Shadows smaller than that, such as the thin dark line where a wheel meets mud, fall between texels. The camera's own depth buffer has a pixel's resolution, so tracing towards the sun in screen space finds them.

The trace (Bend Studio's screen-space shadows)

The pass is Bend Studio's screen-space shadow method (from Days Gone; Apache 2.0, a modified copy ships in the DLL). The sun is projected to a point on screen. Every pixel lies on a line through that point, so the screen is cut into lines radiating from it, and one wavefront of 64 threads walks 64 neighbouring pixels of one line together, sharing the depths they read. For each pixel, 60 depth samples are read towards the sun along its line: a contact shadow can be up to 60 pixels long. A sample occludes when it lies in front of the ray from the pixel towards the sun, every sample standing for a surface of a set thickness (ContactThickness, 0.005 in the pass's depth units); a contrast of 4 sharpens the result. The first 4 samples count as hard shadow, the last 8 fade out, and the result is a mask m from 1 (lit) to 0 (shadowed). The pass reads the game's linear depth as 1/z and skips pixels beyond 50 m (ContactMaxDepth).

Into the picture: only the sunlight

A screen-space shadow must not darken light that is not sunlight. So the patch (module contact) makes each of the 1269 sun-receiving material shaders write the sun visibility it found (the shadow map's lit fraction after the game's floor, g_fShadowAtten) to render target 6. After the lit pass the DLL draws a full-screen pass that multiplies the colour by

k = 1 − (1 − m) · share · strength · fade
share = sun / (sun + sky),   sun = L(Csun) · mpbr · max(0, N · L) · v,   sky = L(the three sky colours blended by Ny)

with v the visibility from render target 6, Csun the game's sun colour and mpbr its PBR multiplier, L() the luminance, N the shading normal (render target 7 where a material wrote it, else rebuilt from the depth buffer). A pixel the sun lights fully darkens to about what a shadow-map shadow would give there; one already in shadow or facing away is not touched. With distance the effect fades: fade = 1 − smoothstep(30 m, 50 m, z). Far away a contact shadow is smaller than a pixel, and ground seen at a flat angle starts to shadow itself in screen space.

Limits

  • Screen space only: an occluder outside the picture, or hidden behind something nearer, casts nothing.
  • The thickness is one number for everything: very thin things (wires) may cast too long a shadow behind them.
  • F4 switches the pass off and on.

Headlight shadows

Card: Headlight shadows, on by default. The truck's headlights cast shadows: a post, a tree or a rock in the beam leaves the ground behind it dark.

How the game lights with its headlights

The headlight is a projected spot light. CB_GLOBAL_SCENE holds its position (g_vHeadLightPos), direction, cone, distance falloff and colour, and the matrix that projects a world position into the lamp's light pattern (g_tmHeadLightViewProj). Each of the about 5100 lit pixel shaders that take the headlight measures the pixel from the lamp, tests the reach and the cone, fades the light out within 3 to 6 m of the lamp and applies the distance falloff and the colour. The objects' shaders (about 2600) also sample the pattern texture g_txHeadLightMap once; the terrain's (about 2300) work out the intensity alone and multiply the lamp's colour by it. Nothing in either chain asks whether anything stands between the lamp and the pixel: the game has no shadow map for its headlights.

The walk to the lamps

The patch (module headshadow) splices a helper in right after the pattern sample and multiplies the sample by what the helper returns, or, in the terrain's shaders, right before the lamp's colour multiply and multiplies the intensity by it: v, the share of the light that arrives. For a pixel at P and a lamp at L the helper walks from 0.12 m off the surface towards L and stops 2.5 m short of the lamp (the truck's own body stands there, and the game has faded its light out that close anyway). Both ends are projected with the previous frame's view-projection (cb1 c10..c13), as the depth they are compared with is that frame's, and the walk takes one step per 6 pixels of its length on the screen, at least 24 and at most 64, evenly spaced, so a pole wider than the gap between two steps is not stepped over (a thinner one can be: see the limits). With a fixed 24 steps a long walk jumped over a fence's rail a few pixels thin, and its shadow came out as a comb of bars, the worse the wider the field of view (seen in the game, 2026-10-27). A step's distance from the camera is interpolated as 1/w:

w(f) = 1 / lerp(1/w0, 1/w1, f),   f = (i + j) / n

with j an offset per pixel (interleaved gradient noise, Jimenez 2014) that the game's TAA averages out. At each step the scene's linear depth z on that pixel (SnowRunner Shadows' copy of the previous frame's depth, t121, taken at the AO pass, which comes after the lit pass) is compared with w:

d = w − z − (0.04 + 0.006 w),   b = saturate(d / 0.15) · [d < 1.5 + 0.03 w] · e

d > 0 means the scene stands in front of the step. More than the thickness (1.5 m plus 3 % of the distance) in front, the ray passed behind the caster, not through it. e fades the test out over the last 3 % of the screen at its border. The lamp's visibility is 1 − max b over the steps.

What blocks is placed in the world: on the camera's line through the step, at the scene's depth there, O = eye + (S − eye) · z / w, with S the step's own position. An obstacle less than 0.3 m in front of the lamps' plane does not count: nothing behind a lamp can stand between it and a lit point. Without that rule the truck's own cab and roof rack, seen from a camera above and behind, lay within the thickness in front of the road and darkened the beam next to the truck (seen in the game, 2026-10-23).

A low headlight's rays leave the ground at a flat angle: for the first metre they run a few centimetres above it. There snow's small lumps blocked them, at a different point for each pixel's offset: a fine grain over the whole lit snow, with stripes on some slopes (seen in the game, 2026-10-26). Each ray now starts 10 cm above the surface, straight up (HS_LIFT), so lumps lower than that do not block. In the test scene with 12 cm drifts and 4 cm roughness and no casters, the share of the beam read as blocked is 0.01 % from behind the truck and 0.02 % from the chase camera, against 5.1 % and 5.2 % without the lift. A second change tried with it, letting the farther of the depths one pixel row above and below count where a step seemed blocked (HS_ROWS, still in the code), cleared the rest in the test scene but cut thin rails' shadows into combs, so it is off.

Nor does an obstacle within 2.5 m of a lamp (HS_BODY, the same as the walk's stop). The walk never comes that close, so what the camera sees there is the truck's own front (a bumper, a winch, a bull bar), found only where it stood in front of a step further out. From a low view beside the bumper it blocked every ray passing behind it: a hard-edged dark quad on the road, its upper edge level with the bumper's (seen in the game, 2026-10-24). In the test scene's view beside the bumper the rule lights 14,272 of 175,948 beam pixels that were blocked, from beside the truck 1,585, and changes nothing from behind or from the chase camera; the posts, the guardrail, the wall and the pole cast as before. In the game the same rule also took away hard-edged dark patches on the road ahead of a truck climbing a hill, which looked like the road's own mesh casting (seen in the game, 2026-10-24).

The first builds projected the steps with this frame's camera. With the camera still that is the same; with the camera moving (shaking on a rough road) each step met whatever the previous frame had at that spot on the screen, and the road ahead went dark until the camera settled (seen in the game, 2026-10-23). With the previous frame's camera a step meets the depth that camera saw there; O is still placed from this frame's eye, a frame's movement off, which moves it by that movement times (1 − z / w), a few centimetres.

The walk starts on the surface the camera saw, at the pixel's own point: where the pixel's camera ray meets the depth. The splice hands the helper the pixel's place on the screen (the shader's own SV_Position: 3,822 of the 5,123 shaders read it already, 1,300 get it declared, as their signature names it), and the helper reads the depth at that pixel. The depth is the last frame's, exact while the camera stands still; while it moves, the point is reprojected into the last frame twice and moved along the ray by what the depth there says. A point the last frame did not see (the depth stays more than 15 cm plus 2 % of the distance off) keeps the game's own light for that frame.

The position the shader is handed is not used for that. On the terrain it is not on the surface the camera saw: on lit snow a little off at every pixel, on a snow bank ahead of a truck metres off in every direction. Each walk then started inside or just above the depth image and met its own steps, which drew a grain and stripes over the snow, worse without TAA, and on the bank a large dark patch with straight edges (seen in the game, 2026-10-27). The first version of the helper did not have them only because it shadowed no terrain: the terrain's shaders, which light the headlight without its pattern, came a day later. Moving the shader's position onto the depth along its own line of sight (1.1.1's first attempt) left the stripes, since that position does not project to its own pixel. Starting from the pixel's own ray, the stripes and the grain were gone at the same spot, with TAA and without. The one shader whose signature has no screen position still moves its position onto the depth that way.

A truck's two headlights stand about 1.4 m apart, and the game hands its shaders one position between them. The helper walks to a point 0.7 m to each side, level and across the lamp's direction, and averages the two, so a pixel that only one lamp reaches gets half the light: a shadow's edge comes out as a half-lit band, the wider the nearer its caster stands to the truck. That is at most 128 depth reads for a pixel in the beam, and none outside it, since the splice sits inside the game's own test of the reach and the cone.

Which shaders

The helper needs the pixel's world position as the shader measured it from the lamp: an input from the vertex shader, or a temporary register, taken only where no instruction overwrites it before the helper runs. 5123 of the 5143 shaders take it; the other 20 (particles whose position comes without its third component, or none at all) keep the game's light. Each patched shader gets its camera constants declared up to the previous frame's view-projection (c13), and t121 declared. The grass's and the bushes' shaders (267) take the headlight but declare no camera constants at all; the patch declares them (b1, which every pixel shader of the game that uses it binds to CB_GLOBAL_CAMERA). Until 2026-10-23 they were left out, and a post or a guardrail standing in a verge cast nothing, while a road cone on the asphalt did (seen in the game): their shadows fall on grass. The module is patched last, on top of what the other modules made of each shader. All 5123 pass the build's input check and are created by Direct3D 11, on WARP and by the GPU's driver (checked offline, 2026-10-23). The first build reached the objects alone: in the game a post's shadow fell on the pole behind it but not on the ground, which is how the terrain's own way of lighting was found.

Limits

  • Screen space only: a caster outside the picture, or hidden behind something nearer the camera, casts nothing, so a shadow can change as the camera turns.
  • Nothing within 2.5 m of the lamps casts: the truck's own bumper, winch and bull bar do not, and neither does a post that close.
  • Thin casters can still be missed: where the walk is longer than 384 pixels on the screen, its 64 steps lie further apart than a thin pole is wide. A walk over the pyramid of the nearest depths (t125, the reflection pass's), which cannot step over anything, is the next step if the game still shows such gaps now that the grass takes the shadows (see Which shaders).
  • A raised overlay's far edge can shade a short band of the ground behind it, as a step that high would (see the start of the walk).
  • Grass is drawn several times over where it is thick, and each layer in the beam now walks to the lamps: there the helper costs more than the times below.
  • The game has slots for four headlights; only the first is shadowed.
  • Without SnowRunner Shadows, or from 250 ms after its copies stop (a menu), t121 is unbound and the light is the game's own.
  • Seen working in the game once (a post's shadow on the pole behind it, against F8); not tuned yet.

Testing without the game

An offline test scene (test\headshadow_scene) ray-casts a synthetic night scene per pixel: ground, a truck body at the lamps, posts, a pole, a concrete block, a low block 1.6 m ahead of the bumper, a wall, a guardrail on posts along the left of the road and a thin marker post; OVERLAY_H raises the depth of a patch of road above its shaded pixels, as an overlay does. It writes the scene's linear depth as the DLL's t121 holds it, fills the camera and headlight constants as the game does, and shades the scene with the helper itself, from beside the truck, behind it (the chase camera), above, low in front of it beside the bumper and high behind it further off (the last two as in the game's shots of the dark patch). Each setting can be overridden there. Seen in it: the shadows fall away from the lamps, doubled up close; with the chase camera the truck's own cab blocks nothing; at 1.5 m instead of 2.5 m the low block casts too and the truck still blocks nothing, which a real truck's bull bar or winch may not share.

Its measure.ps1 times the helper alone, against the same pass without it, one sample per pixel as in the game, the fastest of 60 draws on an RTX 4080: at 3840 × 2160, 0.19 ms with 5 % of the screen in the beam (the chase camera), 0.52 to 0.84 ms with 15 to 23 %, 1.2 ms with the camera low behind the lamps and 39 %. With at most 24 steps, as in 1.1.0, the same views take 0.05, 0.26 to 0.35 and 0.54 ms: the longer walks cost about twice as much. In the game the lit shaders run once per surface drawn, so places drawn over several times (grass, foliage) pay more. Measured in the game at 3840 × 2160 on an RTX 4080 (the lit pass's GPU time, the truck parked with the beam filling the view ahead): 4.6 ms without the shadows, 9.0 ms with them, so about 4.4 ms; with the camera turning, the frame rate stays where it is parked, as nothing is redrawn.

Ambient occlusion: GTAO and the wide reach

Cards: GTAO (row: half or full size), Wide occlusion. Shade in corners, under trucks and in wheel arches, where less of the sky reaches.

What occlusion measures

Ambient light comes from the whole sky. A point in a corner sees less of it. The quantity is the visibility V: the share of the hemisphere above the surface that is open, weighted by the cosine to the normal (light from straight above counts most):

V = (1/π) ∫Ω visible(ω) (N · ω) dω

The game's own pass estimates this with 8 depth samples on a fixed kernel whose size is a fixed share of the screen, whatever the distance: a box looks occluded the same whether it is 2 m or 200 m away.

GTAO: integrating horizons

Ground truth ambient occlusion (Jimenez et al. 2016, "Practical Real-Time Strategies for Accurate Indirect Occlusion"; the integration follows Intel's XeGTAO, MIT) splits the hemisphere into slices: planes through the view direction, at screen angles φ. In each slice the open sky is the arc between two horizons, h1 on one side and h2 on the other: the steepest angle to any surface within a radius. The integral over that arc has a closed form. With n the angle of the normal projected into the slice and |np| the length of that projection:

a(h) = ¼ (−cos(2h − n) + cos n + 2h sin n)
V ≈ (1/S) Σslices |np| (a(h1) + a(h2)),   AO = V1.6

The numbers: 4 slices per pixel, 8 depth taps per side of each slice (64 depth reads), a radius of 1.5 m, capped at 12 % of the pixel's depth so the kernel stays bounded on screen near the camera. The normal is rebuilt from the depth buffer. The slice angles and tap positions are rotated per pixel by interleaved gradient noise; the game's own 3 × 3 blur in its apply pass averages the noise out. The effect fades out between 150 m and 300 m, where fog takes over. The field of view comes from the game's camera every frame. The pass replaces the game's two SSAO shaders and keeps their inputs and outputs: x = visibility, yzw = the centre depth for the blur.

Half size

With the GTAO row at half size (and SnowRunner Shadows' AOHalf = 1) the pass runs on a quarter of the pixels, each half-size pixel snapped to the depth of one full-size pixel it covers, and the DLL upsamples the result weighted by depth, so occlusion does not bleed across object edges. The result is also copied into the game's own AO target: at half size the pass never drew there, and a reader the DLL's substitution did not reach drew the last frame made at full size, a faint picture of an old view that stayed on the screen wherever the camera turned (players' reports, 1.1.1). Full size is the default since 1.1.2.

Wide occlusion

Big shapes (a truck's body, a roof, a tree crown) shade the ground further than 1.5 m. The wide reach continues each search past the near radius to 6 m, with 4 more taps per side, capped at 50 % of the depth near the camera. A far occluder does not count fully: at the near radius it raises the horizon half way (strength 0.5), fading to nothing at 6 m, so the shade under a truck is soft and the crisp contact occlusion near the ground stays as it was. To save time each 2 × 2 quad of pixels shares the far search: each pixel searches one of the four directions far and the four results are shared. That is a quarter of the far reads: 0.9 ms instead of 3.3 ms at 3840 × 2160.

Limits

  • Screen space: what is outside the picture or hidden behind something casts no occlusion.
  • Thin things in front of a wall make a dark halo on the wall; the depth weighting and the fade keep it small.
  • F11 compares GTAO with the game's SSAO, F5 switches between half and full size.

Bounce light

Card: Bounce light (off by default, experimental). Sunlit ground and paint light up what stands next to them, in their colour.

Idea: the horizons already know who blocks the sky

The GTAO search finds, in each direction, which surfaces hide the sky. A surface that hides part of the sky sends its own light instead. Each tap that raises the horizon from h to h' hides a further arc of the sky, and that arc now sees the surface the tap found. Its share is the same cosine-weighted measure GTAO integrates, a(h') − a(h). So the bounce light is

B = s · (1/S) Σslices, sides Σtaps that raise the horizon |np| (a(h') − a(h)) · min(Ctap, 64)

with Ctap the tap's colour in the lit image of this frame (the DLL's copy, t120), clipped at 64 to drop sun glints, and s = 4 the strength. A pixel boxed in by sunlit snow gathers the snow's light; one next to a red truck gathers red. (The derivation is our own, from the GTAO arc integral; the idea is that of horizon-based indirect lighting.)

Keeping a feedback loop stable

The bounce light becomes part of the lit image that next frame gathers from: light can bounce again and again. Two caps keep the loop bounded. The stored value never exceeds 0.9 times the brightest surface it was gathered from. And the material side never adds more than a set multiple of the brightest of its own ambient colours.

Into the materials

The game lights shade with three ambient colours: from above, from the horizon and from below (cPosY, cMidY, cNegY), blended by the normal and multiplied by the surface colour. The patch (gi_ambient) adds the bounce light to all three, so the game's own code carries it on with the real albedo. Because the bounce was measured last frame, each pixel looks it up where it was then: projected with last frame's camera if the world stood still, or at the same screen position if it moved with the camera (the player's truck). Last frame's depth decides which applies; where neither matches (just uncovered), no bounce light this frame. The AO pass stores the bounce divided by the darkening the AO apply pass will give the pixel, so after the apply the bounce keeps its strength.

Limits: it is experimental because in places (bright snow next to dark wood) it comes out too strong, and like every screen-space effect it knows only what is on screen. F10 switches it off and on.

Sky ambient and fill light

Cards: Sky ambient, Fill light. The colour and the amount of the light that fills in shade.

Sky ambient

Each daytime state sets the three ambient colours as one warm grey at three strengths, so shade has no colour variation: under a blue sky it should lean blue, at sunset warm. The patch (sky_ambient, spliced at the start of every material shader that reads those colours) keeps each colour's brightness and takes the hue of the sky in its direction from the reflection cube the shader already binds, at its roughest mip (close to the sky's irradiance): straight up for cPosY, straight down for cNegY, the mean of four horizontal directions for cMidY.

c' = lerp(c, L(c) · S / L(S), 0.6)

with c the authored colour, S the cube's colour in that direction and L() the luminance. The game's own blend by the normal then works on the new colours.

Fill light

The daytime states give shade a lot of light: on a sunny day in Zurdania the light from above is × 3.9 against the sun's × 5.15. Compared with neural-rendered shots of the same scenes, what those darken is exactly this fill. The card scales the three ambient multipliers of every day, dusk and dawn state in initial.pak (199 states; night, garage and menu states stay) by one factor f. Because the fill is about half of all light even on sunlit ground, less of it darkens the whole picture and the game's eye adaptation would lift everything back. So each state's exposure is scaled too:

ambient × f,   HdrExposureScale × f0.45   (f = 0.7 → 0.852; 0.85 → 0.929)

That holds sunlit parts about where they were and leaves shade darker. The edit is made in initial.pak by the engine (see the engine chapter).
