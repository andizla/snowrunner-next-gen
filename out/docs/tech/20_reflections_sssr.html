Object reflections: the reflection pass (SSSR)

Card: Object reflections, with SnowRunner Shadows. Paint, glass and chrome mirror the scene around them. The name SSSR comes from AMD's FidelityFX Stochastic Screen Space Reflections, whose ray walk the pass adapts.

What the game does without it

A glossy material in SnowRunner reflects one thing: a cubemap, a picture of the sky in six square faces, the same for the whole map. The material looks it up in the direction of the mirrored view ray and blurs it by roughness through the cubemap's mip chain: mip level = √roughness × 8 + 0.5. A truck's hood therefore shows a painted sky even when a wall stands next to it. Screen-space reflections add what the camera can see.

The principle

For each pixel we know its position P, the direction to the eye V and the surface normal N. A perfect mirror sends the view ray on in the direction

R = 2 (N · V) N − V

If that ray hits something the camera also sees, the colour of the hit is the reflection. The scene the camera sees is known as two pictures: the lit colour and the depth of every pixel. So the search is a walk along R across the screen, comparing the ray's depth with the depth stored at each pixel it passes, until the ray goes behind a surface.

Where normal and roughness come from

The game draws its world in one pass where each material computes its final colour; it keeps no separate buffer of normals. Next Gen's module sssr splices a small writer into all 3229 vehicle and prop material shaders, at their cubemap sample: it writes the shaded normal (world space, × 0.5 + 0.5) and the perceptual roughness r to an extra render target, RT7 (RGBA8), which the DLL binds beside the lit pass and clears to "up, fully rough" after it. A pixel with r ≥ 0.999 traces no ray.

Straight lines on screen, and why 1/z

The ray from O = P + N (0.002 z + 0.01) (lifted off the surface by 1 cm plus 0.2 % of its distance z, so it does not hit itself) is projected to screen coordinates at both ends. A straight line in the world stays a straight line on screen, but its depth z does not change linearly along it; the reciprocal 1/z does. So the pass keeps 1/z everywhere: the ray is o + t d in (u, v, 1/z), and the depth pyramid stores 1/z too. A larger 1/z means nearer.

The depth pyramid (Hi-Z)

Level 0 holds 1/z of every pixel (0 for the sky, z ≥ 3000 m, so no ray meets it). Each coarser level halves the size and keeps, for every cell, the largest 1/z of the four (or, at an odd edge, six or nine) cells below: the nearest surface anywhere in the cell. A ray that is nearer than that value cannot hit anything in the whole cell.

The walk

The ray starts at level 0 and moves to the far edge of the pixel it starts in. Then, at most 80 times (SSRSteps):

  1. Read the cell's nearest surface s at the current level.
  2. Find where the ray leaves the cell: the nearest of its crossings with the cell's two edge lines and, while the ray moves away from the camera, with the plane 1/z = s. That is tmin.
  3. If the ray is in front of the cell's nearest surface (s < its own 1/z), move it to tmin. If it left through an edge rather than through the surface plane, go up one level: the next cells are bigger. Otherwise go down one level.
  4. Stop when the level drops below 0: the ray stands behind a surface at single-pixel size. Or when it leaves the screen.

A walk over open ground passes many pixels in a few steps; near geometry it slows to pixel steps. This is the method of Uludag's Hi-Z tracing, in the form AMD wrote for FidelityFX SSSR, whose functions FFX_SSSR_InitialAdvanceRay, FFX_SSSR_AdvanceRay and FFX_SSSR_HierarchicalRaymarch the pass adapts (MIT licence, notice in the DLL).

Is it a real hit?

A point behind the depth buffer is not always on the surface: the buffer only stores the front of things. The pass checks, at the pixel where the walk ended:

TestRule
on screen, and movedthe hit lies inside the screen and more than one pixel from where the ray started
not skystored depth zs below 3000 m
a front facethe hit surface's normal Ns faces the ray: Ns · R ≤ 0
thicknesshow far behind the surface the ray stands, b = zray − zs, against the assumed thickness of things T = 0.3 m × (1 + 0.02 zs)

The thickness gives the hit a confidence c between 0 and 1:

c = (1 − smoothstep(0, T, b))²

A ray more than T behind the front went under the object: under a cab, past a load on a bed. Leaving those pixels empty would let the material fall back on the painted sky, which looks like bright patches beside real reflections. So within 2.5 m (plus 5 % of the depth, SSRUnder) behind the surface, the ray takes that object's colour, darkened towards half (SSRUnderShade 0.5) over half a metre past T. This stands for the underside the camera cannot see. It applies in full to surfaces up to 25 m away and fades out by 50 m (SSRUnderNear, SSRUnderFar). It is not used behind a "speck", a small bright thing with nothing around it, such as a firefly or a distant lamp: on at least three of four sides, 2 % of the screen height away, the depth differs and the pixel is three times brighter than its brightest neighbour.

The confidence then fades at the screen's border (over 5 % of its height), with roughness, and for rays turning back towards the camera, whose targets no picture holds:

c ×= saturate((rmax − r) / (0.2 rmax)) × saturate(2 (R · −V + 1)),   rmax = 0.85

Roughness: the cone

A rough surface reflects a blurred picture. By default every ray is the mirror direction, and roughness only blurs the colour taken at the hit, from the feed's mip chain. The blur grows with the distance the ray travelled on screen, d (pixels), and with α = r²:

mip = log2(1 + d · α · 0.5)   (SSRCone = 0.5)

Optionally (SSRLobe = 1) rays follow the GGX distribution itself: each frame draws a different microfacet normal H from the distribution of normals visible from V (Heitz 2018), and reflects about H instead of N. Two uniform numbers u1, u2 (interleaved gradient noise, Jimenez, shifted per frame) become a point on a disc that is stretched by α and lifted onto the hemisphere. Only the lobe's core is used (u1 scaled by 0.85) to keep rare far-off rays from making speckles. This is the "stochastic" in SSSR; it is off by default because the mirror direction with the cone blur is steadier in SnowRunner's motion.

Fewer rays: one per 2 × 2 block

With SSRHalf = 1 (the default) the trace runs on a grid of half the width and height. Each cell traces one pixel of its 2 × 2 block, and the chosen pixel rotates over four frames in the order (0,0), (1,1), (1,0), (0,1), so every pixel gets its own ray every fourth frame. That quarters the trace's cost.

The resolve: from rays to a picture

The resolve runs at full size. Each reflecting pixel gathers the results of the 3 × 3 trace cells around it, since a neighbour's ray stands for a parallel ray from a nearby point. Each one is weighted by distance, depth and normal, so nothing crosses an edge:

w = exp(−|Δp|² / σ²) · saturate(1 − |Δz| / (0.03 z + 0.02)) · (Ns · N)16,   σ² = 2 s² (1 + 8 r²)

where s is the grid step (2 with SSRHalf). From the weighted samples come the mean μ and the spread σ (standard deviation) per channel.

Then the history. The pixel is found where it was last frame: by the camera's reprojection, or, on a moving truck, by the object motion decoded from the game's own velocity buffer. Last frame's result there is used only if last frame's depth agrees: within 2 % + 5 cm for still things, 10 % + 30 cm along a moving object. The history is then pulled toward this frame's mean until it lies within the samples' spread (variance clipping, Salvi 2016). All channels move by the same factor, so the result stays a blend of colours that are actually there:

e = max(1.5 σ, 0.01 + 0.02 |μ|),   m = max over channels |h − μ| / e,   if m > 1: h = μ + (h − μ) / m
result = lerp(μ, h, k),   k = lerp(0.6, 0.9, saturate(r / 0.3))

Mirrors keep little history (k = 0.6); rough paint, whose rays are noisier, keeps more (up to SSRTemporal = 0.9). Where no ray landed near a pixel this frame, its history fades by 0.9 per frame. The output, t123, holds radiance × confidence and the confidence (RGBA16F).

Back into the material

The pass ran during the previous frame, so the material shader reprojects its pixel into that frame with last frame's view and projection, and checks last frame's depth there (3 % + 5 cm; 15 % for pixels a moving object passed). Then it composites, with a the confidence:

colour = reflection + cubemap × (1 − a)

faded by a gloss gate: in full up to cubemap mip 2 (very glossy), the cubemap alone from mip 7 (roughness about 0.67). Reflected light brighter than four times the cubemap's light is compressed, so a lamp in the reflection does not burn out. Glass is a special case: the pass sees the cab behind the window, not the window. Where the depth the pass saw lies clearly behind the fragment (1 % + 2 cm + 1.5 pixels of the fragment's own depth slope), the material marches its own short ray instead (the per-material method of the older Object reflections build).

Cost, limits, artifacts

  • Cost: the resolve takes 0.21 ms at 3840 × 2160 (measured; it is bound by its full-size reads and writes). The trace is a quarter-size pass with up to 80 steps per ray. Per-pass timings can be logged with SSRTiming = 1.
  • Only what is on screen can be reflected. Things outside the picture, behind the camera, or hidden behind other things are not in the depth buffer; the reflection fades to the cubemap there, most visibly at the screen's edges.
  • The thickness is a guess: thin poles may be missed; a ray may pass under a thin object and catch the ground behind it.
  • One frame of delay: fast camera turns show the reflection catching up for a frame, and newly revealed pixels have no history.
  • F6 in the game switches the pass off and on; with it off, the material marches its own ray (or keeps the cubemap).
