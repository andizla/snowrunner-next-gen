Water: reflections, Fresnel, depth colour, crest glow

Cards: Water reflections (row Sun glitter in shade), River depth colour, Sun glow through waves. The game draws water with about 200 shaders: 20 for rivers and mud, 176 for lakes and seas ("domain" water), 88 of those with a planar reflection image. Next Gen changes what they reflect, how reflection and body colour mix, and how deep water is coloured.

Screen-space reflections on rivers and plain lakes

The river and mud shaders, and the lakes without a planar image, reflect only the sky cubemap. The patch replaces their one cubemap sample with a ray march (ssr.hlsl, after McGuire and Mara, "Efficient GPU Screen-Space Ray Tracing", JCGT 2014, simplified for water, whose rays leave the surface upwards). Unlike the object pass it marches in view space with steps that grow geometrically, which suits long reflections over flat water:

stepk = 0.05 m × 1.25k,   k = 0 … 35   (the last step is 0.05 × 1.2536 ≈ 150 m)

The first step is small so a wheel standing in a puddle is found. At each step the ray's point is projected and its depth compared with the scene's linear depth (g_txZ). When it passes behind a surface, 5 halvings between the last point in front and the first behind find the crossing. A hit counts when the ray is at most 0.25 m behind the surface (deeper, it went under the object); the bias is 0.02 m plus a share of the distance. The colour comes from the opaque scene the game already has (g_txBBOpaque). Hits brighter than 3 times the reflected sky are compressed, so lamps do not burn out; the reflection fades over the outer 8 % of the screen. Where the ray leaves the screen or finds nothing, the sky sample stays exactly as before.

The lakes' planar image

88 lake, sea and puddle shaders reflect an image the game renders for one water plane, at half the frame's size, with its own screen-space pass (SpinTires/WaterCompute, "Reflections" and "ReflectionsWithTAA"). For every water texel that pass walks up the screen one texel a step, finds the first thing standing out of the water, and takes the colour twice as far up. Its hit rule has a flaw: it asks that what it finds rises at least minRise = 0.01 + 0.04 s metres per texel (s grows with the depth). An upright thing 20 m away rises 3.0 cm per texel at 1080 rows but only 1.5 cm at 2160 rows, so at 3840 × 2160 a hull, a tyre or a pier standing in the water is found only beyond about 75 m, and the lake shows sky where a truck should be. The patch (patch_refl_hits.js) makes the rule independent of resolution:

minRise = 0.5 × (the height one texel covers at the sampled depth − tol)

On top, the same ray march as the rivers runs at the planar sample (ssr_planar.hlsl). Where it hits, it replaces the planar image (which leaves out the trucks); where it finds nothing the planar sample stays. A ray that ends well behind a surface is not handed to the planar image: under an object it takes that object's colour, darkened, as the object pass does.

How reflection and water colour mix

Light at a water surface splits: a share F is reflected, the rest enters the water. F follows Schlick's approximation of Fresnel's equations, with F0 = 0.02 for water:

F = F0 + (1 − F0)(1 − cos θ)5

The game's river shaders end with out = reflection · F + colour, adding the reflection on top of the full body colour. That works while the reflection is bright sky; with real reflections of darker banks the water turns milky. The patch (blend.hlsl) makes the colour give way:

out = colour · (1 − F) + reflection · F

except for the sun's highlight, which the larger variants add into the body colour and which is reflected light already: its share, highlight · (1 − T), stays as it is.

River depth colour: coloured absorption

The game fades the riverbed behind the water into the water's body colour with one grey factor, T = exp(−k · path), so deep water goes grey at the same rate in every channel. Real water absorbs some wavelengths faster (Beer-Lambert): clear water loses red first and deepens to teal, muddy water loses blue first and goes brown. The patch (absorb.hlsl) gives each channel its own exponent, shaped by the water's own colour c (its lit body colour with the sky's tint divided out):

relc = c / max(c),   ac = 1 − ln(max(relc, 0.02)),   wc = ac / L(a)
τ = −ln T,   τ' = τ² / (τ + 0.7),   Tc = exp(−wc τ')

The brightest channel gets a = 1 and darker ones more; the weights average 1 by luminance L, so the overall depth the artists set stays and only its hue changes. A grey water colour gives the stock fade. The second line clears the shallows: thin water (small τ) lets the bed show much further, deep water fades as before.

Sun glow through waves

Looking towards a low sun across a river, the thin tops of waves glow: light enters the back of a wave and leaves through its crest. The term is the "fast translucency" of Barre-Brisebois and Bouchard (GDC 2011): the direction to the sun, bent by the wave's normal, against the view direction:

glow = 0.15 · Csun · S · hue · crest · saturate(V · −(L + 0.5 N))6

with S the sun's shadow-map visibility at the pixel (looked up as the game's materials do; the 12 river shaders that read the shadow map get it), hue the water's own colour, and crest a weight that rises as a facet tilts away from flat (a tilted facet is a thin crest; factor 4). It is added outside the reflection, as light that came through the water.

Sun glitter in shade (row, off by default)

The game's highlight term on water ignores part of the sun's shadow: water in shade still glitters a little. The row multiplies the highlight's power term, in the 64 lake and 8 river shaders drawn with the shadow map, by

g = saturate(1 − (1 − S) / max(1 − f, 0.001))

with S the shader's own shadow test and f the game's shadow floor (g_fShadowAtten): where the test says the sun is hidden, the highlight goes out. Where it was tried, the game's test said the water was lit, so nothing changed; it stays off.

Limits

  • Screen space: what is off screen is not mirrored; the reflection fades at the screen's edges.
  • The march samples in steps: very thin things (a rope) can be stepped over at a distance.
  • The glow needs the shadow map; 8 of the 20 river shaders do not read it and get no glow.

Wet ground reflections and the glare cap

Cards: Wet ground reflections, Headlight glare cap.

Reflections in mud and puddles

The terrain lights its reflections from the sky cubemap alone (scaled by the local ambient light), so a puddle next to a truck mirrors the sky and nothing else. Terrain is drawn before the game has an image of the scene, so the patch (puddle_ssr, in 1208 wet-ground shaders) reads the previous frame's, which the DLL copies at the AO pass and binds at t120 (colour) and t121 (depth). The ray is marched in world space and projected with the previous frame's camera, so the depth and the colour it meets belong together; for a moving truck the reflection lags one frame. Only glossy pixels march: the cube's mip level, √roughness × 8 + 0.5, must be below a limit. Rough mud takes its colour from the feed's mip chain, blurred like the cubemap would be. Without the DLL (or with F9) every pixel keeps the cubemap sample.

The headlight glare cap

The terrain lights every surface with a GGX highlight per headlight. Its distribution term D is capped at 32 by the game: min(D, 32). On the smooth, rippled surface of a road puddle that cap is reached along a long stretch in front of the truck, and the lamps draw a long bright band across the water. The patch (patch_glare.js, about 1216 shaders) lowers the cap for the headlights only:

min(D, 32) → min(D, 6)   (the headlight terms; the sun's and the baked lights' keep 32)

A cap belongs to a headlight when a read of that lamp's colour (g_cHeadLightColor[k]) lies between it and the previous cap. The number is rewritten in place. Rough surfaces, whose highlight never gets near the cap, look as they did.

Headlights in reflections

Card: Headlights in reflections. At night, paint that finds nothing to reflect shows the light of the headlights.

Where a reflected ray from the object pass finds nothing on screen, the material falls back on the sky cubemap: at night, a dark blue. But the ray passes through the headlights' beams, which light the air (the game draws that as its own beam). This build of the object reader adds, along the reflected ray, the beam's light in the air: the cone, reach and colour come from the game's own constants (CB_GLOBAL_SCENE), so a hood at night shows the lamps' warm light where its rays cross the beam. With the lamps off, or the pass unbound, the result equals the plain reader's at no cost. With two lamps on it adds 0.15 ms for a whole 3840 × 2160 screen of glossy pixels (measured on an RTX 4080); a real frame has far fewer.
